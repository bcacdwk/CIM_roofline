\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
The textbook Roofline model~\cite{williams2009roofline} relates hardware capability to workload demand through the Arithmetic Intensity ($\AI$), and exposes the bottleneck arising from a mismatch between software and hardware. It describes the capability limits on computation and on data supply by the compute capability $\pi$ and the memory bandwidth $\beta$, respectively. $\AI$ represents the number of operations a workload supports per byte of data transferred; comparing it with the hardware ridge point $\AI^*=\pi/\beta$ distinguishes the memory-bound region from the compute-bound region and provides theoretical guidance for optimization~\cite{verhelst2025roofline,gerogiannis2025deca}.

The validity of this model rests on an assumption implicit in the von Neumann architecture: memory and compute are separate units~\cite{rheindt2020xcentric}. Fig.~\ref{fig:operand-roles}(a) shows the familiar picture. Storage and computation are carried out by different hardware, and although activations $A$ and weights $W$ play different roles in the algorithm, the hardware treats both simply as operands delivered to the compute units: neither has a distinct identity, and the two are interchangeable.

However, this assumption no longer holds in Compute-in-Memory (CIM), a non-von Neumann architecture~\cite{sun2023fullspectrumcomputinginmemory}. In Fig.~\ref{fig:operand-roles}(b), the CIM macro that stores $W$ also calculates $A$, so the two hardware units are unified~\cite{verma2019imc}. The \textbf{\emph{weights}} take part in computation as \textbf{\emph{resident state}}, while each new \textbf{\emph{activation}} enters through the read path and \textbf{\emph{streams across}} the macro, where it is calculated against that state. CIM turns one class of operands, the weights, into stored state at the very site of calculation, while the other class, the activations, flows through the macro as inputs served by that state. The separate hardware units of memory and compute in CPU/GPU disappear, but new roles of streaming and resident operands emerge in CIM.

This change calls for a new Roofline model that rethinks how compute capability and data transfer relate in CIM. The need has two roots. First, the hardware fusion of the CIM architecture dissolves the concept of compute capability $\pi$: the capability of a CIM macro to complete multiply-accumulate (MAC) is, in essence, the ``transfer rate'' of $A$. Second, the split of operand roles creates two distinct throughputs: weights enter through the write path, which erases, programs, or refreshes the cells, whereas activations enter through the read path, which applies them as inputs and accumulates their products with the weights as outputs. Hence, whereas $\AI$ captures the GPU bottleneck as an imbalance between computation and memory access, the CIM bottleneck is the imbalance between these two operand throughputs. Prior studies have recognized this problem~\cite{jia2022imc} and proposed several Roofline analyses for CIM~\cite{krishnan2021imc_limits,garofalo2022imc_cluster,sun2023imc_benchmark,meng2024cimdla,verhelst2025roofline,sharma2025wwwtocim,zhou2025imcsim,liu2025ar_llm}, yet none of them reveals this difference.

Therefore, we propose a resident-streaming Roofline model for CIM. Within an explicit workload window, the model separately counts the input bytes $Q_S$ consumed by streaming calculation and the write bytes $Q_R$ that update or reload resident state, and defines the Streaming Intensity $\SI=Q_S/Q_R$ as the streaming demand supported per byte of resident write. The resulting demand--throughput pairing corresponds directly to the classical Roofline: the streaming throughput $\rho$ plays the role of $\pi$, the resident throughput $\tau$ that of $\beta$, and $\SI$, like $\AI$, links the reuse behavior of a workload to the two hardware throughputs. By explicitly distinguishing the two roles of operands in computation, streaming $A$ and resident $W$, the model turns the performance imbalance between resident updates and streaming calculation into a throughput bound and a critical reuse requirement.

Building on this model, we carry out a quantitative analysis from two sides: hardware capability and algorithm demand. On the hardware side, we characterize the streaming and resident throughputs of ten CIM technologies through NeuroSim simulations calibrated with parameters extracted from the literature. On the algorithm side, we examine the matrix multiplications in mainstream large language models (LLM) and characterize the demands posed by static model weights and by the dynamic key-value (KV) cache. This Roofline model provides clearer data-reuse requirements and hardware design guidance for heterogeneous computing across CIM technologies.
